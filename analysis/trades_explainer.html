TRADES: A Plain(ish)-Language Explainer

What this project is, the maths behind it, and what we are trying to do.

Written to be read end-to-end, but it is fine to stop after Part 1 for the gist; the later parts add technical detail. Equations use standard maths notation and should render as proper symbols in a browser — but each one is also explained in words right beside it.

Part 0 — The one-paragraph version

Finance wants a realistic simulator of a stock market — a sandbox to test trading strategies or study how prices move without risking real money. TRADES is a recent (2025) approach that builds one using the same family of AI models behind image generators like Stable Diffusion, called diffusion models. Instead of pixels it generates a stream of market orders (buy this, cancel that) one at a time and lets them play out in a simulated market. The catch: it is slow — each order takes ~100 internal steps. My project is about making it faster. When I swap in the standard fast shortcuts, the simulated market freezes (the price stops moving). The work has been figuring out why that happens, and testing fixes.

Part 1 — The thing being simulated: a limit order book

1.1 How a market matches buyers and sellers

When you buy or sell a share you are matched against specific orders other people placed. Those resting orders live in a structure called the Limit Order Book (LOB), with two sides:

  • Bids — people willing to buy, each at a price and quantity. The highest bid is the best bid.
  • Asks — people willing to sell. The lowest is the best ask.
        SIZE    PRICE
ASKS     300  $33.97   ← best ask (cheapest offer to sell)
         500  $33.98
         200  $33.99
        ------------------  ← the "spread" gap
BIDS     400  $33.95   ← best bid (highest offer to buy)
         600  $33.94
         100  $33.93
  • Mid-price — the fair "current price," halfway between best bid and ask: $$ m = \frac{a_1 + b_1}{2} = \frac{33.97 + 33.95}{2} = 33.96 $$
  • Spread — the gap; a measure of liquidity: $\; s = a_1 - b_1 = 0.02$.

1.2 The three things you can do

Every market event is one of three order types:

  1. Limit order — "buy 400, but only at \$33.95 or better." It rests in the book, adding liquidity.
  2. Market order — "buy 400 right now at the best price." It executes immediately, crosses the spread, and is what actually moves the price (it eats the best ask, so the next-best becomes the new best).
  3. Cancellation — remove a resting order.

Depth measures how far inside/outside the book an order sits, in price ticks (a tick = \$0.01 = 100 integer units). For a buy: $$ \text{depth} = \frac{(\text{best bid}) - (\text{order price})}{\text{tick}} $$ A resting buy limit below the best bid has positive depth (deeper in the book); an aggressive/marketable buy that crosses to hit the asks has negative depth. Negative depth is the mathematical signature of a price-moving order. Hold onto this — it matters later.

1.3 Why simulate this?

A realistic simulator lets a bank ask "what if?" safely. The established academic tool is ABIDES, an agent-based simulator where you script trader-bots by hand: laborious, and only as realistic as your rules. TRADES' new idea is to replace the hand-written traders with one AI model that learned realistic behaviour from real market data.

Part 2 — The engine: diffusion models

2.1 The core idea

A diffusion model learns to create data by learning to remove noise. Two directions:

Forward process (fixed, not learned): take a real data point $x_0$ and add Gaussian noise over $T$ steps until it is pure static. At any step $t$ there is a closed form — you can jump straight to the noised version: $$ x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon, \qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) $$ $\bar\alpha_t$ slides from ~1 (barely noisy) to ~0 (all noise). TRADES uses $T=100$ steps and a cosine schedule $\;\bar\alpha(t) = \cos^2\!\big(\tfrac{t/T + s}{1+s}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big),\ s=0.008$.

Reverse process (learned): train a network $\varepsilon_\theta$ to look at a noisy $x_t$ and predict the noise that was added. If you can predict the noise you can subtract a bit and step back toward $x_{t-1}$, repeating until you recover clean data. The objective is just "predict the noise": $$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, t, \varepsilon}\Big[\, \big\| \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t, c) \big\|^2 \,\Big] $$ where $c$ is the conditioning — the context the model may look at (Part 3).

2.2 The denoising equations in full

The maths that makes this exact. By Bayes' rule the true reverse step, if you knew the clean $x_0$, is itself a Gaussian $\;q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_{t-1};\ \tilde\mu_t,\ \tilde\beta_t I\big)\;$ with a mean that blends the clean and noisy points, $$ \tilde\mu_t(x_t,x_0) = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 \;+\; \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_t, \qquad \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t. $$ We do not know $x_0$ when sampling, so we estimate it from the predicted noise (rearranging the forward equation) — the model's running guess of the clean order: $$ \hat{x}_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t,c)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}. $$ Substituting gives the DDPM sampling step actually run: $$ x_{t-1} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\!\left(x_t - \frac{1-\alpha_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta\right) + \sigma_t\, z, \qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I). $$ That final $\sigma_t z$ is fresh injected randomness at every step. How much to inject is the crux. TRADES uses the improved DDPM (IDDPM) trick of letting the network also predict the variance via a learned per-coordinate knob $v_\theta$: $$ \Sigma_\theta(x_t,t) = \exp\!\big(v_\theta \log\beta_t + (1-v_\theta)\log\tilde\beta_t\big). $$ So the network has two heads: a noise head $\varepsilon_\theta$ and a variance head $v_\theta$.

2.3 What the network predicts is a choice

Three mathematically-equivalent targets differ only in numerical stability: $\varepsilon$-prediction (TRADES' choice — output the noise, recover $\hat x_0$ above), $x_0$-prediction (output the clean point), and $v$-prediction (a blend that behaves well across all noise levels). The fast solvers in §2.5 lean on the $\hat{x}_0$ view because it is more stable at very few steps.

2.4 Why learning to denoise learns the whole distribution

This is the conceptual heart, and not obvious: why should a pile of "guess the noise" regressions reconstruct a complex probability distribution?

The link is score matching. Define the score of the noised data as the gradient of its log-density, $\nabla_{x}\log q_t(x)$ — a field that at every point says which way to move toward higher probability. The optimal noise-predictor is that score, up to a known scale: $$ \varepsilon_\theta(x_t,t) \;\approx\; -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\nabla_{x}\log q_t(x_t). $$ Equivalently, Tweedie's formula says the best denoiser is a distribution-aware quantity — the posterior mean of the clean data given the noisy observation: $$ \mathbb{E}[x_0\mid x_t] = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t + (1-\bar\alpha_t)\,\nabla_x\log q_t(x_t)\Big). $$ So training a denoiser secretly estimates the score of the data at every noise level. And a standard result (the reverse-time SDE / probability-flow ODE) says: following that score field from a simple Gaussian back down the noise ladder transports the Gaussian exactly onto the data distribution. In words:

  • At high noise, the score encodes coarse structure — which broad region plausible orders live in.
  • At low noise, it encodes fine detail — the local shape of the distribution.
  • Composing all levels turns "sample easy Gaussian noise" into "sample from the true, complicated, multi-modal distribution of realistic next-orders."

Why bother, versus directly writing down $p(\text{order}\mid\text{history})$? Because that distribution is wildly complex and multi-modal (the next event could be a tiny cancel, a passive limit, or a market-moving sweep) and has no simple formula. But the denoising task at a fixed noise level is an ordinary regression the network can nail, and stacking those regressions yields an exact sampler for the intractable distribution. That is the whole trick — and it is also why how you sample (§2.5) can make or break which parts of the distribution you actually reach.

2.5 Two ways to run the reverse process (this matters enormously)

  • DDPM (original, stochastic): all 100 steps, re-injecting noise each step (the $\sigma_t z$ term, with IDDPM's learned $\Sigma_\theta$). Faithful, but slow.
  • DDIM / DPM-Solver (fast, deterministic): drop the injected noise, treat the reverse process as a smooth ODE, take big jumps — 10 steps instead of 100, a 10× speed-up. A DDIM step: $$ x_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\hat{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}-\sigma^2}\,\varepsilon_\theta + \sigma z, \qquad \sigma = \eta\cdot\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}. $$ The dial $\eta$ (eta) sets randomness: $\eta=0$ is fully deterministic (no $z$ at all), $\eta=1$ ≈ DDPM.

The one-sentence tension: deterministic samplers are fast because they stop injecting randomness — but for this problem that randomness turns out to be load-bearing.

Part 3 — What TRADES specifically is

TRADES ("TRADES: Generating Realistic Market Simulations with Diffusion Models," Berti, Prenkaj & Velardi, 2025, arXiv:2502.07071) applies the Part-2 machinery to generate one order at a time.

3.1 Encoding an order into a vector

Diffusion operates on continuous vectors, but an order mixes a category (its type) with numbers (size, price, timing). TRADES packs each order into one fixed vector: $$ x_0 = [\,\underbrace{\Delta t}_{\text{time gap}},\; \underbrace{e_1,e_2,e_3}_{\text{type embedding}},\; \text{size},\; \text{price},\; \underbrace{\text{dir}}_{\pm 1},\; \text{depth}\,] $$ Two tricks make heterogeneous fields diffusable:

  • Numeric fields are z-scored (standardised) with dataset statistics $(\mu,\sigma)$, so each sits on a comparable $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ scale Gaussian diffusion likes: $\; z_{\text{depth}} = (\text{depth} - \mu_d)/\sigma_d,\ \mu_d \approx 1.38,\ \sigma_d \approx 2.68$ (similarly size, price, $\Delta t$).
  • The categorical type is embedded: three anchor vectors (limit / cancel / market) live in a 3-D space, and an order's type field is set to its anchor — so "type" becomes three continuous coordinates the diffusion can move through smoothly.

3.2 Decoding a generated vector back into an order

  • Numbers are de-standardised and rounded back to integers: $\;\text{depth} = \operatorname{round}(z_{\text{depth}}\sigma_d + \mu_d)$, and likewise for size, price, $\Delta t$.
  • Type is decoded by nearest anchor: whichever anchor the generated $(e_1,e_2,e_3)$ is closest to (L1/L2 distance) wins, $\;\text{type} = \arg\min_k \| (e_1,e_2,e_3) - \text{anchor}_k \|$.
  • Direction is the sign of its coordinate.

Note for later: to decode as a price-moving order the depth must come out negative, which needs the model's latent draw to fall into the lower tail ($z_{\text{depth}} \lesssim -0.5$).

3.3 The closed loop — why it is a simulator

The model is conditioned on recent history $c$ (the last few hundred orders and order-book snapshots): each new order answers "given what just happened, what is a realistic next order?" And its own output feeds back as the next input:

   history c  ──▶  diffusion model  ──▶  new order x₀  ──▶  apply to the order book
       ▲                                                             │
       └──────────────  book + order become the new history  ◀───────┘

Run this thousands of times and a full trading session emerges. Because it is a feedback loop, small biases compound: if the model systematically under-produces price-moving (negative-depth) orders, the simulated mid-price gradually freezes.

Part 4 — Accelerating TRADES: the problem, and what we are trying

4.1 The goal

The paper's model works but is slow: ~100 network evaluations per order, times thousands of orders. My thesis is about accelerating it — getting the same realism from a ~10-step deterministic sampler (a 10× speed-up) so the simulator is practical to run.

4.2 Trying faster solvers, and what breaks

So we swapped the slow stochastic DDPM (100 steps) for fast deterministic solvers (DDIM, DPM-Solver, DPM-Solver++, UniPC — 10 steps, $\eta=0$). The market freezes:

unique mid-prices / sessionbook behaviour
Real market~69prices move continuously
DDPM (slow, stochastic)~100healthy, moves
DDIM (fast, deterministic)~9–17 frozen; giant "walls" of stuck orders

The price stops moving and the book piles up huge volumes (40,000+ shares vs a realistic ~2,000). Notably, using a more accurate deterministic solver does not help — which is the first clue that this is not solver error.

4.3 Investigating why

Three linked facts explain the freeze:

  1. A few-step deterministic sampler collapses to the average. With randomness off and only a few big jumps, the reverse process integrates toward the conditional mean of what comes next — and the average order is a small, passive limit order.
  2. Price-moving orders live in the tail, and the tail needs randomness. Recall a price-moving order needs negative depth, i.e. a latent draw well below average ($z_{\text{depth}} \lesssim -0.5$). The per-step noise in stochastic DDPM regularly produces such draws → occasional aggressive orders → the price moves. A deterministic sampler sits at the mean and essentially never dips negative → no aggressive orders → frozen price. DDPM works precisely because its noise keeps re-sampling that tail.
  3. The diversity is decided early. We confirmed this with a controlled test: putting the randomness in the early (high-noise) steps un-freezes the market, whereas adding it only in the late steps does not. Once the early steps have collapsed to the mean, no amount of late-stage noise recovers the lost variety.

We also checked and ruled out the boring explanations — it is not a start-step misalignment, not out-of-distribution input prices (DDPM runs fine on the identical conditioning), and not a flipped convention. And a "sharpen the conditioning" knob (classifier-free guidance) does not fix it either, which is expected: guidance reshapes the mean; it adds no variance, and this is a missing-variance problem.

Where the investigation lands: the freeze is a variance phenomenon centred on the depth channel. Under deterministic sampling the depth output collapses to a near-constant that sits right on the boundary between passive and price-moving — so the market simply never crosses the spread.

4.4 What we tried, and what we are trying now

(A) A "churn" sampler — add back just enough randomness (no retraining). Tried; negative result. Since the diversity is set in the early steps, we used the fast, accurate deterministic solver for most steps but on the first few deliberately re-injected noise (an idea from the "EDM" diffusion literature), controlled by a strength dial $\kappa$: $$ \hat{\bar\alpha}_t = \bar\alpha_t\,(1-\kappa), \qquad \hat{x} = \sqrt{\tfrac{\hat{\bar\alpha}_t}{\bar\alpha_t}}\,x_t + \sqrt{1 - \tfrac{\hat{\bar\alpha}_t}{\bar\alpha_t}}\; z. $$ In words: nudge the sample back to a slightly noisier state before denoising, only on the steps that matter, by a tunable amount. $\kappa=0$ is the pure fast sampler; turning $\kappa$ up re-introduces the tail-exploring randomness. We swept it across five checkpoints and two strengths. The result: no combination lands near real. Every cell is either still frozen (under 2% marketable orders) or suddenly overshoots into a hyperactive, diverging market (35–47% marketable, far more unique mid-prices than the real session) — the same cliff-not-dial shape we had already seen with a depth-scaling knob, just relocated onto a different control. Sampler-side tricks (a guidance-scale knob, a depth-scaling knob, sampler-ordering hybrids, and now this) have now all hit the identical wall: a binary frozen/diverging outcome with no controllable realistic middle.

(B) The current experiment — a data hypothesis: does training ever show the model a price-moving order? Because every sampler-side lever hits the same wall, the next hypothesis moves upstream, to what the model was trained on. One preprocessing detail stands out. When the real data is prepared for training, a single line clamps depth to be non-negative, $\;\text{depth} \leftarrow \max(\text{depth}, 0)$, which would erase the negativity of every aggressive order before training begins. If so, the model is never shown a price-moving order as a target and can only ever reproduce aggression as sampling noise — which would neatly explain why removing the noise removes the movement, and why every noise-shaped fix (guidance, depth temperature, hybrids, churn) hits the same cliff rather than a controllable dial. The experiment, now running: retrain on the honest, signed depth and see whether even a fully deterministic fast sampler then moves the market. If it does, the freeze was a data-preparation artifact rather than a limitation of fast diffusion samplers.

4.5 Why this is worthwhile either way

  • Churn's negative result is still useful: it closes off the entire family of sampler-side fixes and sharpens the case that the problem is upstream, in the training data.
  • If unclamping works: we show that a published model's most dramatic failure under acceleration is really a one-line data-preprocessing artifact, and exactly how to fix it — a clean, cautionary result about how invisible preprocessing choices silently constrain what generative models can learn.

Glossary

LOB (Limit Order Book)
the ledger of all resting buy/sell orders for a stock.
Bid / Ask / Spread
best buy price / best sell price / the gap between them.
Mid-price
midpoint of bid and ask; the reference "current price."
Limit / Market / Cancel order
rests in the book / executes immediately (moves price) / withdraws.
Depth
how far into the book an order sits; negative = aggressive, price-moving.
Diffusion model
an AI that generates data by learning to reverse a noising process.
Forward / reverse process
adding noise to data / removing it to create new data.
Score / score matching
the gradient of the log-density; denoising secretly estimates it, which is why it can reconstruct a whole distribution.
Tweedie's formula
the identity that the best denoiser equals the distribution-aware posterior mean of the clean data.
ε- / x₀- / v-prediction
equivalent choices of what the network outputs.
IDDPM / learned variance
letting the model predict how much noise to re-inject per step.
DDPM
the slow, stochastic sampler (re-adds randomness each step) — realistic here.
DDIM / DPM-Solver / UniPC
fast, deterministic samplers — freeze here.
η (eta)
DDIM's randomness dial; 0 = deterministic, 1 = DDPM-like.
Conditioning (c)
the recent market history the model may look at.
Closed loop
feeding the model's own output back as its next input, making it a simulator.
Churn / κ
our method of re-injecting a tunable amount of randomness into a fast sampler.
Depth clamp
the max(depth, 0) preprocessing line — the leading hypothesis for the freeze, to be tested next.
ABIDES
the agent-based market simulation framework TRADES runs inside.
TRADES
the diffusion-based market simulator this project accelerates (arXiv:2502.07071).
